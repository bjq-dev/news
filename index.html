    1. Google's Gemini 4 Hydrogen: the fast everyday model that punches above its weight
    Source: https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/gemini-models/introducing-gemini-4-hydrogen/
    Google has released Gemini 4 Hydrogen, the quick, inexpensive member of its new model family, and unlike the slow, security-first rollouts of Carbon and Argon, this one goes out widely on day one: in Google Search for everyone, in the Gemini app, and for developers. The surprise is how capable the "small" model is: on a popular independent intelligence score it roughly matches Google's heavyweight Argon, and Google says it makes things up about five times less often than the Flash model it replaces. Alongside it comes an Orchestrator in Google's Antigravity coding tool that switches between the brainy and the speedy models mid-task, like a manager handing routine jobs to the fastest worker. Caveat: the comparisons are Google's own.

    2. A 22-year-old Postgres flaw turned into full server takeover
    Source: https://mehmetince.net/part-3-6-how-i-ended-up-turning-a-22-year-old-postgresql-type-confusion-primitive-into-rce-cve-2026-6460/
    Security researcher Mehmet Ince tells how a question about managed databases led him down a months-long rabbit hole into the heart of PostgreSQL. The trick is almost playful: put a value inside a bundled "composite" type, change the type of one of its fields, then ask the database to read the old value back. Postgres gets confused about what it is holding, and that confusion became one of the most reliable memory-corruption exploits he has seen, letting him run his own code on the server. The weakness dates back to 2004. He learned the internals with an AI agent as a tutor, and many teams, including AI labs, reported overlapping variants. Patched releases are out (CVE-2026-6460); update and rebuild extensions.

    3. AlphaGenome 2 reads DNA the way individual cells do
    Source: https://deepmind.google/blog/alphagenome-2-deciphering-how-cells-read-dna-to-better-understand-disease/
    Every cell in your body carries the same DNA, yet a heart muscle cell and an immune cell switch on very different genes. Google DeepMind's AlphaGenome 2 is built around that idea: instead of predicting averages over whole tissues, it takes a specific cell type as an extra input and predicts how a tiny spelling change in the DNA would play out in that cell. It finds roughly twice as many gene-changing variants as its predecessor, can adapt to a new cell state from a small sample of a few dozen cells without retraining, and reproduced a known gut-inflammation finding on its own. It is for non-commercial research only and is not approved for clinical or diagnostic use.

    4. Apify breached through a single stolen login
    Source: https://status.apify.com/
    Apify, a popular marketplace for web scrapers and AI agent tools, says an intruder got into internal systems on 9-10 November using one compromised credential. The attacker may have viewed account, billing and two-factor recovery details; Apify says passwords were not exposed. The company cut off access, rotated API tokens and internal secrets, revoked some third-party integrations, and is asking affected users to reset two-factor authentication and reconnect GitHub integrations. The lesson is old but keeps coming back: in a world of agents and automations wired together with tokens, one leaked login can open many doors. If you use Apify, check your email and rotate anything that touched it. Coverage: https://www.bleepingcomputer.com/news/security/apify-rotates-api-tokens-prompts-2fa-resets-after-breach/

    SCIENCE

    9. Webb sees a "forbidden" glow from the universe's most important little molecule
    Source: https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.10122
    H3+, three hydrogen nuclei sharing two electrons, is the quiet engine of chemistry between the stars, but its perfect triangular symmetry means it should barely shine at the colours that reveal how it spins. Forty years ago theorists predicted a faint loophole: as the molecule spins, it distorts slightly, letting a whisper of light escape. Using the Webb telescope's mid-infrared camera, a team led by David Neufeld finally caught that whisper, in shocked gas blasted out by a newborn star in the object HH 168, with dozens of clearly detected lines, then found it again in another young star's jet. The puzzle: it is several times brighter than shock models predict.

    10. A dead star flashing every eight minutes, paired with a Sun-like star
    Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41550-026-03016-z
    Australian astronomers using the Murchison Widefield Array found radio flashes arriving like clockwork every eight minutes, which then vanished for weeks. Plotting every sighting revealed the pattern: the pulses only show for about six hours of a roughly day-and-a-third orbit, when two stars line up just right. Follow-up pinned the source to a white dwarf, the dense ember of a dead star, circling a star almost identical to our Sun, the first such radio-pulsing pair with a Sun-like partner. Timing shifts in the pulses weighed the white dwarf, and its beam even passed behind a magnetic loop on its companion, letting astronomers detect storms on a star thousands of light-years away. Release: https://www.curtin.edu.au/news/media-release/researchers-discover-rare-star-system-that-emits-radio-signals-every-eight-minutes/

    Source: https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/gemini-models/gemini-4-carbon-now-available/
    Google's strongest model, Gemini 4 Carbon, has gone from a small circle of cyber-defence teams to every paying developer and top-tier subscriber.     2. Anthropic ships one brain under two names: Claude Fable 5.2 and Mythos 5.2
    Source: https://www.anthropic.com/claude-fable-and-mythos-5-2
    Anthropic released a new top model in two versions that are literally the same underlying system. Fable is the public face, wrapped in safety filters that block risky cyber-attack and biology requests; Mythos is the unfiltered twin, available only to vetted security and life-science teams.     3. AI-assisted hunting finds memory-safety bugs inside Rust itself
    Source: https://forge-blog.github.io/posts/rust-std-memory-safety/
    Rust is famous for promising that "safe" code can't corrupt memory. Microsoft's FORGE Lab, using an AI vulnerability scanner as a research partner, found several places in Rust's own standard library where that promise quietly broke. The bugs fall into three kinds of misplaced trust: assuming code never crashes halfway through, assuming user-supplied pieces behave honestly, and assuming the operating system's shared state stays put. One bug had been hiding for nearly eleven years. The interesting twist is that AI agents seem good at the third kind, because they understand how the surrounding system behaves. Caveat: each bug needs an unusual setup, so it isn't an automatic hole in Rust programs, and two reports are still open.

    4. Gemini Robotics 2.5 puts a humanoid to work on a Mercedes production line
    Source: https://deepmind.google/blog/gemini-robotics-2-5-accelerates-real-world-physical-intelligence/
    Google DeepMind's new robot brain sees, understands instructions and moves hands, and it learns new skills faster than before, from tying a bow on a gift to pipetting liquid. The headline test was real: Apptronik's Apollo humanoid sorted metal car brackets from bulk bins into trays at Mercedes-Benz factories, succeeding almost every time. Part of the speed-up came from using the big Gemini model as a kind of imagination engine, generating thousands of simulated practice runs from a single camera view. DeepMind also released an open test for "jailbreaking" robots with disguised unsafe requests. Caveat: it's going to partners only, and the factory work was a proof of concept.

    SCIENCE

    8. A brand-new way of looking confirms the M87 black hole's ring of light
    Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-026-11241-9
    The famous first picture of a black hole showed a glowing ring around a dark centre, but because the image was stitched together from sparse radio data, some critics argued the ring might be an artefact of the processing. A team in Australia has now rebuilt the picture with a completely independent method that doesn't assume any shape in advance and relies only on measurements immune to telescope calibration errors. The ring came back, clear as before, matching a black hole of roughly six and a half billion suns. It's science doing what it should: checking a landmark result from scratch and finding it holds.

    9. Two monster black holes, side by side, at cosmic dawn
    Source: https://phys.org/news/2026-11-earliest-compact-quasar-pair-poses.html
    Using the Webb telescope and the Gemini North telescope in Hawai'i, astronomers found the oldest close pair of quasars ever seen, two feeding supermassive black holes shining when the universe was under a billion years old. Nicknamed Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua, they are already as heavy as giants we see nearby today, and they sit closer together than the width of our galaxy. That is a puzzle: nobody can fully explain how even one black hole grew that big so fast, let alone two. The team suspects they live in an unusually dense knot of the early universe where colliding galaxies fed them. Paper: The Astrophysical Journal Letters.

    10. An invisible, overweight neutron star tugging on a red giant
    Source: https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/newfound-invisible-object-in-space-could-be-a-rare-supermassive-neutron-star/
    In Pisces, a red giant star is wobbling back and forth as though something heavy is circling it, yet that something gives off no light. After ruling out other options, Chinese astronomers argue it is most likely a neutron star, the city-sized crushed core of an exploded star, but an unusually heavy one, well above the typical weight. The mystery is where the extra mass came from: normally a neutron star bulks up by stealing gas from its neighbour, but this orbit looks too wide for that. Heavy neutron stars are prized because they test the extreme physics of matter inside them. Published in Science Advances; the identification is still an inference.
